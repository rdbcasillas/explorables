    The Climate Bathtub

    Carbon dioxide builds up in the atmosphere the way water fills a bathtub. As long as more flows in than drains out, the level keeps rising, even while the tap is being turned down.

    In a 2007 study, the researchers John Sterman and Linda Booth Sweeney asked 212 graduate students at MIT what would happen to carbon dioxide in the atmosphere under different emissions scenarios. Three in five had backgrounds in engineering, science or math.

    Most answers broke a basic physical rule. Of the students asked to sketch both emissions and natural removal, 84 percent drew paths that violated conservation of mass, and 63 percent showed CO₂ levels holding steady while more carbon entered the air each year than left it.

    The researchers traced the error to a common intuition: people expect a total to rise and fall along with the flow that feeds it. If emissions stop growing, it seems the amount of CO₂ in the air should stop growing too. It doesn't. The total keeps climbing for as long as more is added each year than is removed.

    The sections below test that intuition, first with a tank of water and then with the atmosphere.

    The level depends only on the difference between the two flows. When more water comes in than goes out, it rises. When less comes in, it falls. When the two are equal, it stays at whatever height it has reached, whether that is 50 in and 50 out or 80 in and 80 out.

    04

    The atmosphere as a tub

    The same arithmetic governs carbon dioxide. Think of the atmosphere as the tank, and the amount of CO₂ in it, measured in parts per million, as the water level.

    • The tap is human emissions, from burning coal, oil and gas and from clearing forests. They total about 42 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year.
    • The drain is the ocean and land plants. Seawater dissolves CO₂, and plants take it in as they grow. Together they absorb a little over half of what people emit.
    • The tank itself is the air. Before industrialization the tap and drain were roughly balanced, and the level stayed near 280 ppm for thousands of years.

    The shaded gap between the two lines is the CO₂ that stays in the air each year. Emissions have exceeded natural uptake in every year since 1850, and the level has risen in every year. It averaged 422.8 ppm in 2024, higher than at any point in human history.

    07

    Why the drain slows down

    Natural uptake does not run at a fixed rate. The ocean and plants absorb more when there is more excess CO₂ in the air, and different parts of the system work on very different timescales.

    Years

    The surface ocean and growing plants take up CO₂ quickly, but they also reach their limit quickly.

    Centuries

    The deep ocean absorbs more as surface water slowly mixes downward.

    Millennia

    The remainder is removed by the slow chemical weathering of rock.

    These timescales follow the multi-model analysis by Joos and colleagues.

    This is why, in the previous section, emissions had to keep falling to hold CO₂ steady: the fast sinks were filling up. The chart below shows what happens when emissions stop altogether.

    Once emissions stop, the level starts to fall, but slowly. Centuries later, much of the added CO₂ is still in the atmosphere.

    08

    Warming follows the level

    The amount of heat the atmosphere traps depends on how much CO₂ it holds, not on the rate of emissions. The ocean also takes up heat slowly, so surface warming lags behind.

    The result is fairly simple: warming keeps rising until emissions reach roughly zero, and then it stops rising.

    Why does it stop? When emissions reach zero, two things happen at the same time, and they push the temperature in opposite directions. Step through them one at a time.

    CO₂ falling
    What actually happens

    The two pushes are roughly equal in size, so they cancel out. The temperature stays about where it was when emissions stopped. It stops rising, but it does not go back down.

    Use the slider to choose when the world reaches net zero.

    What this line shows

    Each point on the line is one possible net-zero year. The later the year, the more CO₂ is emitted on the way, and the warmer the world is when emissions finally stop. The line is straight: every 100 billion tonnes adds about 0.045°C of warming, whenever it is emitted.

    That straight line is why scientists talk about a carbon budget, a fixed amount of CO₂ that can still be emitted for a given warming limit. The Global Carbon Budget team estimated in late 2025 that about 170 billion tonnes could still be emitted while keeping a 50 percent chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C. That is about four years of current emissions.

    09

    The cost of delay

    A carbon budget is fixed. Every year that emissions stay high uses up more of it, so a later start requires faster cuts to stay within the same limit.

    Warming limit

    The budgets are the Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimates for a 50 percent chance of staying under each limit: 170 billion tonnes for 1.5°C, 525 for 1.7°C and 1,055 for 2°C. In reality, emissions are not yet falling at all, as the next section shows.

    10

    Where emissions are actually headed

    The scenarios so far have assumed that cuts begin soon. They have not begun. Fossil fuel emissions reached a record 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025, and total emissions, including land use, have stayed near 42 billion tonnes for several years. Over the past decade they grew by about 0.3 percent a year.

    The warming is already arriving. 2024 was the first calendar year more than 1.5°C warmer than the pre-industrial average, at 1.60°C, and 2023 to 2025 was the first three-year period to average above that level. The World Meteorological Organization puts the chance that the 2025–2029 average exceeds 1.5°C at 70 percent.

    The International Energy Agency models where energy emissions go under different assumptions. Under the policies governments have actually put in place, emissions stay near 40 billion tonnes a year through 2050 and never peak. If governments also carry out the policies they have announced, emissions peak soon and fall below 30 billion tonnes by 2050. Choose a path to run it through this page's model.

    The IEA scenarios cover energy emissions to 2050. Here they include about 4 billion tonnes a year from land use. After 2050, current policies are held flat and announced policies continue their 2035–2050 decline. The IEA's own warming estimates include all greenhouse gases.

    What the major assessments project

    Several groups track where today's policies and pledges lead. Their methods differ, but none puts the world on course for 1.5°C. Even if every pledge were met in full, including long-term net-zero targets, warming would reach about 1.9°C.

    Under the most optimistic of UNEP's scenarios, the chance of holding warming below 1.5°C is about 21 percent. In the terms of this piece, the tap is still running as fast as it ever has, the drain takes a little over half, and the tub keeps filling.

    Summary

    1. The amount of CO₂ in the atmosphere rises whenever emissions exceed what the ocean and land absorb, even while emissions are falling.
    2. Holding emissions steady does not hold CO₂ steady. Current emissions are well above the rate of natural uptake.
    3. Warming stops increasing at roughly net zero emissions, and the peak depends on the total emitted before then.
    4. Delay leaves a smaller budget for the years that follow, so the cuts have to be faster.
    5. Emissions have not yet started to fall. Under current policies, the major assessments project 2.6°C to 2.9°C of warming by 2100.

    Sterman and Booth Sweeney argued that public support for waiting to see how harmful climate change becomes may stem less from indifference than from this misunderstanding. Waiting works for a kettle, where there is almost no delay between the whistle and taking it off the stove. In the climate there are long delays at every step, from emissions to CO₂ levels to temperature.

    How the model works

    The charts on this page come from a small model that runs in your browser. It is meant for teaching, not forecasting.

    • Carbon: the four-timescale response of atmospheric CO₂ to emissions from Joos et al. (2013), with timescales scaled (as in the FaIR model) so that the historical record from 1850 to 2025 is reproduced to within about 10 ppm.
    • Heat: a two-layer energy-balance model (surface and deep ocean). Gases other than CO₂ and aerosols are folded in as a fixed fraction of CO₂'s effect. It is calibrated to match human-caused warming of about 1.36°C in 2024 (ref. 6) and to roughly reproduce the IEA's 2025 scenario projections, and warming levels off at net zero, in line with the models reviewed by MacDougall et al. (2020). It runs slightly cooler than the IEA's estimates, which include all greenhouse gases in detail.
    • Emissions history: fossil fuels plus land-use change, approximated from the Global Carbon Budget.
    • Not included: tipping points, the weakening of natural sinks as the planet warms, and separate treatment of methane and aerosols. Most of these would make the real problem harder.
