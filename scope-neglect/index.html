Scope Neglect

Why 200,000 birds don't feel a hundred times worse than 2,000, and what that means for how we give and vote.

This is a great crested grebe, mired in the wake of a tanker spill. It will not last the night. Most people, asked, would pay something to save it.

In 1992, economists ran that question as a real experiment. Migrating birds were drowning in the oil industry's uncovered waste ponds; nets could stop it. People were asked what they'd pay, once, for the nets.

Psychologists call this scope neglect: the felt response to a problem barely tracks the size of the problem. It is one of the most reliably reproduced findings in the study of judgment.

Why the feeling won't scale

When you read "2,000 birds," you didn't picture two thousand birds. You pictured one: the bird at the top of this page. Psychologists call this evaluation by prototype. We judge a category by one vivid mental sample, not by counting, and the prototype doesn't get sadder when the number grows. One drowning bird already fills the frame. Your wallet answers to the picture, not the arithmetic.

Flip that around and it gets darker: if the total feeling stays flat while the number climbs, the concern reaching each individual is collapsing. Studies find precisely this. In 1997, researchers asked people to rate an aid program that would save 4,500 lives at a Rwandan refugee camp. The program seemed far more worthwhile when the camp held 11,000 people than when it held 250,000. Same program, same lives saved; it just felt smaller.

The same curve shows up with people and money. Show donors one hungry child, with a name and a face, and they give. Describe millions of children in the same need and they give less:

Paul Slovic summarized decades of this in five words: "the more who die, the less we care." The line attributed to Stalin, one death is a tragedy; a million is a statistic, describes a real defect in human cognition. Mother Teresa put the same defect to work in the other direction: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will."

What actually helps

The obvious fix would be to explain the bias and hope for the best. Researchers tried that. In the Rokia studies, one group of donors was taught how the identifiable-victim effect works before being asked to give. The lesson didn't transfer any feeling to the statistical millions. It just cooled the feeling for the one child, and giving fell. Whatever the fix is, it isn't awareness, which means reading this far hasn't inoculated you either. The things that work change the shape of the decision itself.

Put numbers side by side. On its own, "200,000 birds" is unreadable; there is nothing to hold it against, so the prototype takes over. Christopher Hsee calls this the evaluability problem, and his studies show the same person who ignores scale when judging one option alone becomes sensitive to it the moment two options sit next to each other. So never evaluate a lone quantity. Find a second one: another charity's cost per life, last year's number, the 2,000-bird version of the same question.

Start from one, then multiply. Charities have tested a technique called unit asking: before asking what you'd give for forty children, ask what you'd give for one. Donors who answer the small question first give far more for the group, because they've set a per-person anchor and their own arithmetic won't let the flat answer stand. You ran this exact procedure on yourself earlier, when you locked in a number for 2,000 birds before seeing 200,000.

Decide the policy before the emergency. Slovic's own conclusion, after decades of this research, is pessimistic about feelings and practical about institutions: if compassion fails at scale, stop consulting it at scale. Fix a giving budget in January. Set the aid formula per person. Write the law so it triggers at a body count. The counting part of your mind makes the decision once, calmly, and the numb moment only has to execute it.

The slowest fix is also the most general: get better with numbers. Ellen Peters' research on numeracy finds that people who are comfortable with rough arithmetic draw more feeling from a number, not less, and resist the numbing curve better. The practice is unglamorous. Estimate things. Ask "per person? per year? compared to what?" until it becomes a reflex. The same habit that resists scope neglect also catches misleading statistics, bad pricing, and inflated risks.

The feeling itself never scales. Two hundred thousand will not feel like a hundred times two thousand, no matter what you do. The workaround is to notice when a question is about how many, and refuse to answer it with the part of your mind that can only see one bird.
