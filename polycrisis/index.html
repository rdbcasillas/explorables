Polycrisis, in brief

We’re often told we live in a “polycrisis”. This page walks through one careful paper on what that word should mean. Start with the picture: switch between crises side by side and crises entangled. The gap between those two pictures is what the paper is about.

Crises that feed each other

A polycrisis is not just many crises at once. It is crises that are causally tangled together.

After 2019 the word spread quickly, through the UN and the World Economic Forum, and got vaguer as it spread. This paper tries to pin it down. It starts from the most detailed existing definition, from the Cascade Institute, then offers its own. Here they are side by side. Tap an underlined phrase to see what it adds.

Cascade Institute · Lawrence et al. 2024

“the causal entanglement of crises in multiple global systems in ways that significantly degrade humanity’s prospects”

Tap a phrase.
This paper · Mark et al. 2025

“a state that encompasses a complex set of risks characterized by multiple, macroregional, and often ecologically embedded linkages between inexorably interconnected systems”

Tap a phrase.

Both definitions share a core idea: causal entanglement. Trouble in one system raises the odds and the damage of trouble in others, as in the entangled picture at the top of the page.

They differ on where the crisis sits. The Cascade Institute puts it in the harm that results. This paper puts it earlier, in the risk. The next chapter shows why that matters.

A crisis starts with risk

To say when a crisis begins, the paper first breaks risk into parts.

Risk is the chance that something goes wrong. The paper builds it from three parts that multiply together: a causal factor that could do damage, the system’s vulnerability to it, and its exposure to it. Try it on a bridge. Move the three sliders and watch the bridge and its risk change.

Risk =××=
lowelevatedhigh

Push any one slider close to zero and the risk stays low, because the parts multiply. A flimsy bridge that storms almost never reach is still a low-risk bridge.

The paper also splits causal factors into two kinds. Stresses are slow, like corrosion eating at a support for years. Triggers are sudden, like a violent gust. They work together: corrosion raises the bridge’s vulnerability, so the next gust does far more damage. Slow stresses magnify sudden shocks.

With risk defined, the paper makes its main move. It defines a crisis as a period of high and rising risk, when urgent decisions are needed to avoid severe harm. The figure below breaks a crisis into three stages and marks where each definition says it starts.

The gap between the two starting points is the window to act. Defining a crisis by its risk keeps attention on that window, and if leaders act in time, stage 3 never arrives.

The paper’s example is the Cuban Missile Crisis. The risk of nuclear war was grave but never realized. By this paper’s definition it was a crisis, handled in time. By an outcome-based definition it barely counts.

A different planet

Pressure on the Earth system now sits under every other crisis.

The authors call human-driven Earth-system stress a foundation of today’s polycrisis. Their evidence is the planetary boundaries framework, which sets safe limits for nine Earth systems. In the chart below, the green disc is the safe zone. Wedges that reach past it have crossed their boundary.

    2023 assessment by Richardson et al., as cited in the paper. Wedge length shows only the status, not how far past the boundary each system is.

    Six of the nine are past the line, and ocean acidification is close. That makes today different in two ways. First, the risk is unprecedented: even crises 20 years ago happened on a fundamentally different planet. Second, it can’t be avoided: the stress is so large that serious harm is coming whatever policies we choose. The open question, the authors write, is how hard the landing will be.

    The definition, assembled

    A polycrisis is continuing feedback between high risk, intense stresses and harmful outcomes, across many interacting systems, at the scale of a continent or the whole globe, usually with the Earth system tangled in.

    Not a new kind of disaster

    Polycrisis doesn’t invent a new disaster. It makes the familiar ones more common and harder to escape.

    Disaster researchers already have names for disasters that combine. Each sketch below shows how the events line up over time, with one real example.

    The first three usually stay in one place for a limited time, and places tend to recover. A polycrisis makes all three happen more often, feed each other and spread across borders and systems, with almost no time to recover in between.

    Why does recovery get so hard? The paper’s Figure 2 shows the same shock hitting two systems: one with no ongoing stress, and one under constant, growing stress.

    If you look only at the shock, you expect the left-hand picture. But stresses like climate change are permanent and still growing, so today looks like the right-hand one. The Cascade Institute calls the habit of watching only the shock trigger fixation. It leads to underestimating the damage.

    Has this happened before?

    Partly. The authors place crises on a spectrum instead of labelling them polycrisis or not.

    At one end of the spectrum is a crisis that stays in one place and one system. At the other is today’s global entanglement. Below, three eras sit on that line. The dots under each show which features of today’s polycrisis it shared.

    Only today combines global connection with permanent, human-made change to the planet. So the authors say we are in a polycrisis rather than the first and only one. The past still holds lessons about what helps, and the last chapter comes back to them.

    What helps: trust and polysolutions

    The paper ends on responses, and on the piece it thinks others overlook: the social fabric.

    Every response to a crisis depends on people and institutions coordinating, and coordination runs on trust. Trust in institutions, science and politics has been falling for decades. The authors argue this is part of the polycrisis itself, because lost trust feeds a loop. Here is how COVID-19 went round it:

    Each turn of the loop makes the next crisis harder to handle. So where does trust come from? The paper points to social capital: the networks, norms and trust that let people act together. It comes in two kinds. Bonding ties link people within a group. Bridging ties link different groups.

    To see the difference, picture a small town with three groups: farmers, health workers and the local council. Each group already has strong bonding ties. The dotted lines are bridging ties that could exist. Tap them to build or cut them, then send a drought and see who hears about it in time to help.

    Acts together · 2 direct ties Helps in time · 1 direct tie Helps late · hears second-hand Never hears

    A drought is never only a farming problem. It soon becomes a health problem and a problem for the council too. Bonding ties help the farmers support each other, but only bridging ties carry the warning, and the help, across groups. A polycrisis crosses systems, so bridging ties are the kind it needs most.

    Bridging ties grow in social infrastructure: libraries, parks, places of worship, community spaces and social businesses. These are everyday places where people meet others outside their own circle. The evidence is concrete. Communities with more of these places have had lower disaster death rates than comparable communities.

    Social infrastructure is also a polysolution, a response that helps several systems at once. Compare it with a fix built for a single hazard:

    A fix for one hazard

    Japan’s sea walls reduce tsunami damage, but they can trap river water and make floods worse. The risk moves to another system.

    A polysolution

    A public library teaches, shelters people from heat waves, offers internet access for job hunting, gives vulnerable people a safe space, and builds bridging ties.

    Farm to School programmes work the same way, linking food, health, local economies and the environment. Single fixes worked better when crises came one at a time. After the Black Death, England’s lower rents and higher wages helped its economy grow. With entangled crises, a fix aimed at one system can shift the risk onto another.

    History offers hope as well as warnings. Here are two societies from the plague-and-cold centuries of chapter 5 that coped better than their neighbours:

    Both prepared ahead and adapted quickly, which is the opposite of trigger fixation. Crises can even leave some good behind. After 3/11, some areas near Fukushima invested more in renewable energy.

    What comes first

    Many democracies face coordinated misinformation campaigns against public health and public goods. Without trust, big social investments won’t win support, so the authors put rebuilding trust first. They call for democratic resilience, built from the bottom up and the top down. They are also realistic: as shocks overlap, they expect partial, temporary recoveries to become the norm.

    To sum up the whole paper:

    1. A polycrisis is crises that are causally entangled across systems, not just crises that happen at the same time.
    2. A crisis starts when risk rises and decisions become urgent, before harm. That keeps the window to act in view.
    3. Human-driven stress on the Earth system now sits under everything, and it won’t go away.
    4. Polycrisis makes familiar disasters more frequent, more connected and harder to recover from.
    5. Trust is part of the problem and part of the answer. The best responses help many systems at once.
