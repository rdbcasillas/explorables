Why do I want to write? Is it because I enjoy the process of clarifying my thoughts on something? Sure. Is that it? No. If I am being honest, I like the idea of having a body of work under my name, having written a book or two, being called a “writer”. Maybe I want to impress people with my ideas? But why do I enjoy fantasizing about all that? Do I crave validation or esteem? Meaning?  We can keep going…

Why do I want to conduct rationality workshops and work on improving education? Is it because I am frustrated by what our education systems have been doing to our minds for over a century? Is it because I believe education is one of the deepest layers that needs to be transformed in order to bring about change at the societal level? Yes, both of those things. But I also like the idea of working on tough civilizational-level problems and then talking to people about it. I hate the idea of doing 9 to 5 like a cog in the machine, the rat race and all that. But why do I hate that? Beneath all this, is there a desire to stand out from the crowd? Is there also some deep desire to “prove to the world” or something? I honestly don’t know.  

So how does one go about figuring out what one really wants? 

Instrumental and terminal goals

To think clearly about this, I find it useful to split my goals into what are called instrumental goals and terminal goals.

Terminal goal - Something you want for its own sake. It is not desired because it leads to something else. Satisfying your hunger is a terminal goal. Wanting to be loved is a terminal goal. 

Instrumental goal -  Something you want because it gets you closer to the terminal goal. If that terminal goal isn’t in the picture anymore, you will likely stop caring about this goal. I don’t know anyone who wants money for its own sake. You want what it allows you to have: security, freedom, comfort, status. Money is the vehicle, those are the destinations. Getting a college degree works the same way - it gets you a job, which then gets you money, which finally gets you the things you actually care about.

Same instrumental goal can also satisfy multiple terminal goals for different people, same I but different Ts

It can be tricky to figure out which of your goals are instrumental and which ones are terminal. And one can get a bit nihilistic about the whole exercise by applying evolutionary principles. Isn’t “spreading your genes” or “survival” the ultimate terminal goal, with everything else being upstream of that? I don’t want to go down that road because it won’t help us change our behaviors or make better decisions (for every animal, everything can be traced back to gene propagation). 

It is useful, however, to scrutinize some of your goals to figure out what you truly want. What’s the root of my primary motivation, something I can actually act on (unlike my genetics)? If I can figure that out, maybe I'll act more efficiently, instead of stacking up instrumental goals. Moreover, it will help me build better mental models of my motivation, instead of mistaking a vehicle for the destination. 

So let me get my hands dirty by breaking down one of my goals. 

Breaking down one of my own

Goal - Play a significant role in transforming education

That’s a bit vague, isn’t it? What do I mean by “significant role”? Do I need to win some big prize in education? Do I need to start a successful school? And what do I mean by “education”? Education of kids? Or do adults also count? “Transformation” is quite a loaded word too. 

This vagueness is itself part of the problem. Vague goals are hard to examine properly as they can subtly hide the real motivation behind impressive-sounding language. This goal as stated can mean five different things and each of those might have different terminal goals behind it. 

But vagueness can also be a useful signal. It means that I want to move in a particular direction. I have an idea about how I want to orient my career but am not sure about the exact destination, which is completely okay! 

Instead of calling it a goal, we can just call it a…direction. I don’t have a goal around education - it is more like a direction. There are different directions one can choose when it comes to having an impact in improving education systems and I want to explore a specific direction. 

Direction is a pre-goal, a felt sense before you’ve figured out concrete goals. But why do I have *this* pre-goal and not some other one? Unpacking that requires me to go back to when I was about 10 or 11.

I still remember my sister telling me something along the lines of “there’s a big chance there’s no such thing as God and it’s all made up.” There was a sense of excitement in hearing that. Excitement about telling my close friends, seeing their reaction, excitement about holding a belief that very few people held.

I enjoyed being a contrarian. That was very likely the main reason I first became an atheist. The logical reasons came afterwards. The original attraction to this belief wasn’t purely truth-seeking. There was something enjoyable in sharing with others that I was an atheist. Maybe some sort of ego boost. Being a contrarian probably made me feel unique in those teenage years.

I suspect that this part of me might still be active. Working on education in a direction that very few people are working on feeds that part of me. If tomorrow the world decided education was worth funding, and smart people flooded the rationality and critical-thinking space, I'd be happy, of course. But my motivation to work on it might dwindle a bit. Maybe. Which means this pre-goal might just exist to satisfy the terminal goal of being a contrarian. Though even that probably isn't the bottom, as being a contrarian is likely itself instrumental, a way to reach something hidden further down: the desire to feel special and unique.

In reality though, the terminal goal is not always going to be a clean bottom layer. Sometimes what looks terminal is just the deepest layer you currently have access to. Regular introspection and therapy are necessary to get access to the deeper layers. 

Whatever the actual terminal goal is - being a contrarian, feeling unique, or something even deeper - the question becomes the same: “Can I find a more direct way to satisfy it, and then check whether I still want to work on education?”

The purpose of all this isn’t to remove the desire for status or recognition or feeling unique. The aim is to notice these motives clearly so they don’t quietly control me from the background. 

Cost of getting it wrong

So what happens if you don’t do this work of getting clarity on what’s instrumental and what’s terminal? It’s possible that you end up building your life around the wrong thing. Because you mistake instrumental for terminal, either you don’t feel content after reaching it or you struggle to find motivation to work on it. A classic example of this is someone wanting to feel secure. They decide making lots of money is the way to do it (this decision usually isn’t conscious). They grind for years, make a pile of money, buy a lot of stuff, but still the anxiety doesn’t go away. The insecurity wasn’t really about money. It was either about relationships or about self-worth or something else. But they built their entire life around earning more. 

The same is possible for someone desiring a feeling that they matter in this vast meaningless universe. When they were a kid, their parents/community never made them feel that they mattered. Enter social media. They start chasing followers, likes, retweets - some visible proof that people care about what they think and have to say. The numbers go up and it feels great. For a while. Then it becomes the new normal and they need more. They start optimizing further for better hooks and thumbnails, studying what goes viral, timing their posts well, etc. The whole thing starts feeling like a job because these metrics become part of their identity. The original desire to feel that they matter is still there underneath. They just end up creating an elaborate system around it that needs constant feeding. 

In both cases, the problem isn’t the instrumental goal itself. Money and audience aren’t by themselves bad things to want. The problem is not knowing why you want them. Without that clarity, you can’t tell whether you are getting closer to what you actually need or just running faster on the wrong path.

When you can't figure out what's missing

CFAR - an organization that designs workshops on improving decision-making - offers another useful frame for this. Pica is a condition where people obsessively crave or eat things that aren’t really food - like ice, dirt, chalk. It has many possible causes, but one of them has to do with nutrient deficiency. Iron-deficient people, for instance, sometimes get a strong urge to chew ice even though ice contains no iron at all. The idea is that the body knows something is missing but isn’t great at identifying exactly what, so it falls back on a rough heuristic and ends up craving the wrong thing.

The CFAR handbook points out that this can be considered a decent metaphor for a lot of what we do. We chase the audience because we want to feel like we matter, or we buy fancy stuff because we want to feel different about ourselves. The behavior in these instances (instrumental goal) is the ice cube. What we actually need (terminal goal) is the iron. 

Sometimes you can quickly figure out what you really want, drop the instrumental goal, and go straight for the actual thing. For example, an iron-deficient person takes an iron supplement and the ice craving stops. But it is often not that simple. Sometimes what you are really missing is hard to get (even after figuring out what). You just have this sense that what you are doing feels a bit hollow. 

So what can be done then? The handbook basically says: stop trying to think your way towards the solution. Instead, pay attention to your actual experience. First, notice when something feels a bit empty. Then try something else and check whether it feels less hollow. If yes, do more of that. Keep iterating and it’s possible to get closer to the right thing even if you never figure out the exact terminal goal. 

Running the experiment

So maybe I can’t simply introspect and figure out whether my fascination with education is about being contrarian or actually caring. But I can try to pay attention to which parts of this journey feel hollow and which ones don’t. 

For instance, I ran a workshop recently where only 10 people showed up. I could sense that everyone was engaged and enjoyed the experience. I felt I did justice to most of the concepts I wanted to cover. But there was still some desire to see more than 10 people, and I felt a bit disappointed. I didn't feel this disappointment when I organized a rationality game for about 50 people. Content-wise, the smaller room was way deeper and more impactful. But the bigger crowd - all those excited faces - gave more instant gratification.

And then there have also been times during my life-coaching sessions, where discovering a new insight about my client’s behavior has been very gratifying, even though there’s no audience or applause. 

This is good data, but it isn’t necessarily clarifying. It's messy, contradictory data about what actually moves me. So what do I do with this conflicting information? 

I can’t keep analysing and hope that I can just think my way to the ultimate terminal goal. One possible move is to run a small experiment. Maybe for the next few months, I deliberately lean towards smaller crowds and deeper work - the 1-1 coaching, the 8-10 person workshops - and completely let go of scaling up. And then just observe. Does the depth keep me motivated by itself? Or do I keep hearing protests from an inner voice that wants a bigger audience? 

Maybe running experiments like this teaches me something new about my motivations. Or maybe I feel clarified for a while and then slide back into chasing instrumental goals. Noticing a pattern doesn’t necessarily mean I will escape it. But it does feel like movement in the right direction - towards a better understanding of what’s actually moving me. 

Faced with such questions about underlying motivations, my instinct is to try to think my way out. But rationality isn't just about thinking harder. It is mostly about building better systems and practices that move you closer to what you actually want.
