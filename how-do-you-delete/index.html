How Do You Delete 13 Crore Voters?

Normally, you stay on the voter list unless someone proves you should go. In the re-check India began in 2025, you go unless you prove you should stay. Voters got about a month to return a signed form, and about one in seven didn't make the draft list.

Since June 2025, India's Election Commission (EC) has been re-checking every voter, in a Special Intensive Revision, or SIR. In the states covered so far, 13.3 crore of about 95 crore names were left off the draft voter lists. Some people later got back on.

Here is how it happened, in five steps. Then comes the harder question: who did it help?

This is the short version. For the history, the people affected and the fight inside the Election Commission, read the full story, Erased from the Roll.

Step 1

Flip the default

The first step is the rule itself. A Booth Level Officer (BLO), usually a local schoolteacher, brings every voter a form. If a signed form doesn't come back within about a month, the name is left off the draft list.

"Absent" only means that no signed form came back. It does not prove that anyone moved.

Step 2

Ask for a link to a list from 2002

Returning the form is not enough. You must also link yourself to the last time this was done, around 2002–2004 in most states. Either your own name was on that old list, or a parent's was. Watch three neighbours try.

No link means a notice: you must prove you are eligible with documents. Here is a typical drawer of papers, sorted by the EC's list.

The voter ID card you vote with does not count as proof that you can vote. This step is hardest for people with the fewest papers: married women whose surnames changed, migrant workers, and tribal and homeless people.

Step 3

Let software flag families

Even a link may not be enough. Software compares each family's details with the old list and flags anything that looks odd. The EC calls these "logical discrepancies", and each one means a hearing. Watch it check one made-up family against three of the real rules.

Big families, early marriages and guessed birth dates all look suspicious to the software. In West Bengal, 60 lakh people were flagged. Judges then reviewed every case, on the Supreme Court's orders.

Step 4

Do it in a rush

BLOs are mostly schoolteachers and anganwadi workers with their own jobs. One BLO's area can hold up to about 1,200 voters. In Phase II, each BLO had one month to reach all of them, collect the forms and upload them on a phone app. Tap through the red button to see what that means for one person.

In a rush, the people who drop off are the ones who were away, busy, or never reached.

Step 5

Make the way back hard

If you are left off, you get about a month to apply to get back on. The way back is Form 6, the form for new voters. You apply as if you had never voted, and sign that it is your first time. A false statement can mean up to a year in jail. Falling off took one step. Climbing back takes four.

Bihar, the first state to finish, shows how few made the climb. Its draft list dropped 65 lakh names. In the month that followed, 21.53 lakh names were added, and that count includes first-time voters. The final list still had 47 lakh fewer names than before the SIR.

Meanwhile, control moved away from local officials. By law, each constituency's Electoral Registration Officer (ERO) decides who is added or removed. Since January 2026, all voter-list work runs on new EC software, ECINet. Two of the EC's three members say this moved control to Delhi. The diagram starts with what the law says. The harder question

Did it help the BJP?

So that is how 13 crore names came off. Many people then ask: was this done to help the ruling party? Here the evidence is thinner, and it points in different directions.

What we know

  • Most removed names were marked "absent", not dead.
  • Results varied hugely by state: about one in three voters removed in Delhi, about one in thirty in Kerala. The EC says cities simply have more people who have moved.
  • Muslims were hit harder in West Bengal and Maharashtra, according to two analyses (chart below).

The chart below shows two states. In each, compare the grey bar with the red bar.

What is not proven

  • That the deletions were planned to help the BJP. No one has shown intent.
  • How the removed people would have voted. The data cannot show it.
  • That the deletions decided who won West Bengal. Three separate analyses say the BJP would have won a majority anyway.

Researchers and reporters have compared deletions with election results. That is only possible in states that have voted since their SIR.

The EC says its rules apply to everyone equally. The Supreme Court ruled in May 2026 that the SIR is legal.

A rule can fall hardest on migrants, poor families and Muslims, and tilt results, without anyone planning it. Unequal harm is not the same as proof of intent.

What would settle it

  • An independent check of a random sample of "absent" names. Are those people really gone?
  • ECINet's records: who removed which names, and from where.
  • Booth-level data comparing deletions with how each booth voted before.

The bottom line

Whoever gained, the way this was done is a problem in itself. A voter away for work, a family whose birth dates were guessed, a name spelled two ways: none of these depends on which party a person votes for. In Uttar Pradesh, the biggest losses were in seats the BJP holds. The bigger question is whether anyone should lose their vote because a form did not come back.

Inside the Commission, two of its three members objected in writing to how parts of this were run, at least 14 times in ten months. The Indian Express reported it on 23 September 2026. That report turned a year-long argument into street protests demanding that the Chief Election Commissioner resign.
