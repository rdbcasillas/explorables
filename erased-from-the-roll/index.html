    Erased from the Roll

    In just over a year, India's Election Commission has removed more than 13 crore names from draft voter lists. Now two of the Commission's three members say important decisions were taken without them. This is the story of how a clean-up of voter lists turned into a demand that the Chief Election Commissioner resign.

    On 2 October, Gandhi Jayanti, protests against the Election Commission were called in several places. In Delhi, student groups and activists called a gathering at Jantar Mantar. They include AISA president Neha Bora, lawyer Prashant Bhushan and RJD MP Manoj Jha. In Mumbai, the Cockroach Janta Party called a protest at Shivaji Park. As of 1 October, police had not given permission in Delhi, and had refused it in Mumbai. The opposition INDIA alliance is also holding "Save Democracy" marches in districts from 2 to 8 October. Its MPs plan to march to the Election Commission's office on 6 October.

    The protests share one demand: Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar must resign. Congress also wants him arrested, and wants an independent check of the voter lists. Opposition MPs are preparing a second attempt to remove him from office.

    Why now? People have been arguing about India's voter lists for more than a year. What turned it into a national story was a newspaper report. On 23 September, the Indian Express reported that two of the Election Commission's three members had objected in writing at least 14 times in ten months. They said decisions about a huge revision of the voter lists had been taken without them. Within days, the opposition, student groups and activists were calling people onto the streets.

    To see why that report hit so hard, this story first goes back a little. It explains how the voter list works, the first fight over it in 2025, and what the revision did to crores of voters. Then it comes back to the report itself.

    Part No. 1

    The list is the election

    Being an Indian citizen over 18 is not enough to vote. Your name must also be on the electoral roll.

    The electoral roll is simply the official list of voters. In Hindi it is called the मतदाता सूची. The Election Commission keeps one for every area of the country. It is updated every year. Names are added when people turn 18 or move in, and removed when people die or move away. Before each election, the roll is printed, and a copy goes to every polling booth.

    At the booth, staff look for your name on a printed page like the one at the top of this story. Your voter ID card helps them find it, but what lets you vote is the entry on the roll. Let's see how this list is organised, and who looks after it.

    One roll per constituency

    India has 4,123 assembly constituencies. The Election Commission keeps a separate roll, or voter list, for each one. India has about 97 crore voters, so on average a roll has about 2.3 lakh.

    Split into parts

    That is too many people for one polling booth. So the roll is cut into smaller parts, one for each booth. A part has up to about 1,200 voters. A typical constituency has around 250 parts. The page at the top of this story is from one part.

    The BLO works on one part

    Each part has a Booth Level Officer (BLO). This is usually a local schoolteacher or anganwadi worker. The BLO goes from house to house. They hand out forms and collect them. They report who has died, moved away, or is listed twice.

    The BLO cannot add or remove a name. The BLO only reports.

    The ERO decides for the constituency

    Above all the BLOs is one Electoral Registration Officer (ERO) for the whole constituency. This is usually a senior local official, such as a sub-divisional magistrate. The law gives the ERO the power to decide who is added or removed. The ERO also hears complaints and publishes the roll.

    Officials at the district, the state and the Election Commission in Delhi supervise the ERO. But by law, the decision on each name belongs to the ERO. This will matter in Part 8.

    Where your voter ID comes from

    When the ERO adds you to the roll, you get a voter ID card. Its official name is the Elector's Photo Identity Card (EPIC). The card's number points to your entry on the roll: a part number, and a serial number in that part.

    The card is not the roll

    The card shows who you are. The roll decides if you can vote. If your entry is deleted, your card still looks the same. But at the booth, there is no entry for it to match.

    That is why this story is about names on the roll, not about voter cards.

    Most years, the roll gets a light update called a "summary revision". Names are added or removed only when someone applies. An intensive revision is different. The old list is thrown out and rebuilt from scratch, house by house. Everyone has to prove again that they belong. In most states, the last one was done in 2002–2004. Now the Election Commission is doing one again, across the country. It is called the Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, and Part 3 explains how it works.

    The key idea: In a normal update, you stay on the roll unless someone proves you should be removed. In an intensive revision, you are removed unless you return a signed form. The job of proving moves from the government to the voter.

    Keep this idea in mind. It is at the heart of the SIR, and of this whole story.

    Part No. 2 · Background, 2025

    The first alarm

    First, some history. A year before the Indian Express report, the voter list had already become a national fight. On 7 August 2025, Rahul Gandhi, the Leader of the Opposition, held a press conference. He showed printouts of the Election Commission's own voter roll for one assembly constituency in Bengaluru: Mahadevapura. He said he had found "vote chori", or vote theft.

    Mahadevapura is an assembly constituency, like the ones in Part 1. It has its own roll, split into parts. For a Lok Sabha election, several assembly constituencies are grouped into one parliamentary seat. Each one is then called a segment of that seat. Mahadevapura is one of eight segments that make up the Bangalore Central seat.

    The map below shows these eight segments. Mahadevapura is the big area in solid violet, on the city's eastern edge.

    Mahadevapura is also the biggest segment by number of voters. It has over 6 lakh voters, nearly a third of the whole seat's 19.3 lakh.

    Congress picked Mahadevapura because of how the seat was won. Counting all eight segments together, the BJP got 32,707 more votes than Congress. That was its winning margin. But in Mahadevapura alone, the BJP got 1,14,046 more votes than Congress, while in the other seven segments put together Congress was ahead by about 81,000. In other words, the BJP's whole win came from Mahadevapura. The chart below shows this.

    Congress then checked Mahadevapura's roll. It said it found 1,00,250 suspect entries. That is about 1 in 6 of the segment's voters, and about three times the BJP's winning margin. The chart below splits these entries into the five types Congress named.

    Gandhi's later claims were not only about names being added. On 18 September 2025, he said someone had tried to delete 6,018 voters in Aland, another constituency in Karnataka, using fake online applications. He said it was caught only by chance, when a BLO noticed her own uncle's name among those being removed. He also said Karnataka's crime investigators had written to the EC 18 times in 18 months, asking for technical details, without getting them.

    On 5 November 2025, Gandhi made a bigger claim about Haryana. He said there were 25 lakh fake or duplicate votes in its 2024 assembly election. In one example, the same photo of a Brazilian model appeared on many different voter cards.

    The Election Commission said the claims were baseless. It said no member of the public can delete a vote online. Gyanesh Kumar told Gandhi to either make his claims in a signed statement under oath, or apologise.

    Some examples also turned out to be less than they seemed. Opposition MPs wore T-shirts showing "Minta Devi" from Siwan in Bihar, listed in the draft roll as a 124-year-old first-time voter. The roll really did say 124. But she is 35. Officials said the person who filled in her form online typed her birth year as 1900 instead of 1990. It was a typing mistake, not a fake voter, and she had already asked for it to be corrected.

    As early as 18 August 2025, the opposition INDIA alliance said it was thinking of trying to remove Kumar. Nothing was proven either way. But both sides agreed on one thing: the voter rolls were messy. These fights were about a few constituencies. What came next touched almost every voter in the country.

    Part No. 3 · June 2025 – today

    The great deletion

    The Commission's answer to messy rolls was already under way. On 24 June 2025, it ordered a Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of Bihar's voter rolls. This was six weeks before the first vote chori press conference. An SIR is a house-to-house re-check of every voter. Instead of correcting the existing roll one entry at a time, it prepares a fresh roll from scratch. The aim was to remove dead voters, people who had moved away, duplicate entries, and non-citizens. After Bihar, the SIR was rolled out across the country in waves.

    The method is the same in every state. The easiest way to understand it is to follow a few voters through it.

    Start · the existing roll

    Here is one booth's roll, cut down to seven people. The people are made up, but each one is a common case.

    Step 1 · The BLO brings a form

    The BLO visits every voter on the roll. They bring an enumeration form, already filled in with the voter's details from the roll.

    Step 2 · Sign it and hand it back

    You check the details, sign the form, and give it back. If a form does not come back, the BLO writes down a reason.

    Sunita works in another city and was not at home. Gopal died last year. Ravi was on the roll twice, at his old address and his new one. He returned the form only for the new one.

    Step 3 · Show your link to the old roll

    With the form, you must also show a link to the last intensive revision. In most states, that was in 2002–2004. Either your own name was on that old roll, or a parent's name was.

    Ramesh was on it himself. Priya's parents were on it. Imran's family was not, so he gets a notice asking for documents.

    Step 4 · The draft roll

    Everyone whose form came back goes on the draft roll. Everyone else is left off. Here, that is three out of seven. Across India so far, it has been about one in seven.

    Step 5 · Claims and objections

    Next comes a period of about a month. Anyone left off can apply to be added back, using Form 6, the form for new voters. Anyone can also object to a name on the list.

    Sunita hears about it and applies. Imran takes his documents to a hearing. The ERO decides his case.

    Step 6 · The final roll

    Gopal and Ravi's old entry stay off the roll. That is correct. Sunita is back, but only because she found out in time and applied herself.

    Voters who don't hear about it, or can't apply in time, stay off the roll.

    That was one booth with seven people. Now let's look at the whole country. The SIR has happened in three waves, which the EC calls phases. Bihar came first, in mid-2025. Then 12 states and union territories followed in late 2025, and 19 more in 2026. Together, these cover about 95 crore voters. About one in seven of them, 13.3 crore people, were missing from the draft rolls.

    A draft roll is not the end. As Sunita's story showed, people who were left off can apply and get back on. Bihar was the first state to finish, so it shows how much this changed the numbers. The chart below follows Bihar's roll on three dates: the start of the SIR, the draft roll, and the final roll.

    Two things stand out. First, everyone added in this period came in through Form 6, the form for new voters. So a person who had been removed had to apply again, as if they had never voted before. Part 7 comes back to this. Second, Phase II followed the same pattern. About 6.5 crore names were missing from its draft rolls, and 5.18 crore were still missing from its final rolls.

    These national numbers hide big differences between states. The same process, under the same orders, removed one in three of Delhi's voters but only about one in thirty of Kerala's. In the chart below, each bar shows the share of voters removed in one state. The longest bar is at the top. The colour shows which phase the state was in. Striped bars are draft numbers, which may still go down.

    The Commission says these differences are real. Big cities have more people who have moved, and more old entries that should have been removed. Some states had also kept their rolls cleaner. Critics disagree. They say one common process should not give such different results from state to state. Phase III removed 17.1% of names in its draft rolls. That is much higher than the 12.3% in the first two phases. On 27 May 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that the SIR is legal. So the debate is no longer about whether the SIR can happen. It is about how it was done.

    Part No. 4

    Dead, moved, or "absent"?

    One way to see how it was done is to look at why each name was removed. Every removed name is given a reason. The SIR was presented mainly as a way to remove dead voters and duplicates. But the biggest group turned out to be a much vaguer one. The bars below take all the names removed in three places. They split them by the reason the BLO wrote down.

    In Delhi, 91 of every 100 deleted names were marked absent, shifted, or "other". Fewer than 6 were marked dead. "Absent" only means the BLO did not get a signed form back. The voter may have moved. They may have been at work, or visiting family. They may not have found the papers to show a link to the 2002 roll. None of this proves that the person cannot vote.

    One study looked at the full lists of removed names for Delhi, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Telangana. It found not a single person marked "Other". That is the reason code for someone who refused, or was not able, to fill in the form. It seems these people were put under "Absent" instead.

    Part No. 5 · West Bengal

    When the software has doubts

    Getting your form back in was not the end of it. Even people who returned their forms could still be flagged, this time by software. West Bengal held its assembly election in April 2026, so its SIR was watched very closely. It is also where most people first heard of a new reason for flagging voters: "logical discrepancy". The EC brought this in for Phase II, so it was used in all 12 states and UTs of that phase. It is being used again in Phase III states such as Telangana and Maharashtra. Software compares each voter's family details with the old roll from around 2002, and flags anything that looks odd.

    About 60 lakh voters were flagged this way. The Supreme Court stepped in. It asked about 250 serving and retired judges to decide these cases. They cleared 32.68 lakh people. They found 27.16 lakh people ineligible to vote. Those people could appeal to 19 special courts, called tribunals, run by retired High Court judges. The chart below follows these 60 lakh voters, from being flagged to election day.

    Only a few appeals were decided before election day. At the speed reported, 19 tribunals would need more than two years to hear 10 lakh appeals. No one can say how many of the 27 lakh would have voted, or for whom. What is clear is that, for this election, the calendar decided their appeals, not the tribunals.

    There is a second pattern in who was flagged. The EC's lists do not record religion. But reporters who sorted the lists by surname found that Muslims were hit far harder than others. In West Bengal, about 7 in 10 people ruled ineligible were Muslim, in a state where just over a quarter of people are Muslim. In the 49 seats where these removals were bigger than the winning margin, the share was 85%.

    The same pattern shows up outside Bengal. The chart below puts the two states side by side. In each panel, the grey bar is the normal level and the red bar is what happened to Muslims or Muslim-majority areas.

    The EC says its rules apply to everyone in the same way. Even Telangana's chief electoral officer got a notice. But a rule can treat everyone the same on paper and still fall much harder on some people. Frontline found that the burden falls heaviest on poorer families, whose lives and papers do not fit the software's idea of a normal family.

    Part No. 6

    The people caught in the middle

    Behind these numbers are two groups of people who carry the weight of the SIR. The first is the BLOs: the schoolteachers and anganwadi workers from Part 1 who go door to door. The second is voters who do not have the right papers.

    In Phase II, BLOs got one month, from 4 November to 4 December 2025, to reach every voter in their part, collect the forms and upload them on a phone app. Many still had their regular jobs to do. Here is what that month cost.

    The EC asked states for reports on the deaths and offered "additional support". On 4 December 2025, the Supreme Court told state governments to give the EC more staff, to ease the pressure on BLOs. In the same hearing, the EC said criminal cases had been started only against BLOs who refused to do the work.

    The strain showed up again in Phase III. In Hyderabad, Frontline found some BLOs posted 10–15 km from their homes, in areas they did not know, using an app full of glitches. Most rarely visited homes at all. They worked from community centres instead, and many residents never got their forms or notices.

    The second group is voters without papers. Take Shahida (not her real name), 29, from Hyderabad. She and her six siblings each got a "logical discrepancy" notice, because seven children were linked to the same father: rule 1 in the list above. Each of them must now go to a hearing and prove who their father is.

    The figure below compares how many documents different people have, and how many officials asked for. Each square is one document.

    The people with the fewest papers are often the ones asked for the most. Shahida's siblings are spending their daily wages to get new documents. In Telangana, the list of people who got notices was not put online until 8 September, weeks after notices went out, and several thousand people missed their hearing dates. The state's chief electoral officer confirmed that the notices were "AI-generated".

    Other groups hit the same wall. Married women whose surnames have changed struggle to prove their link to their parents in old rolls. Migrant workers were far from home when the BLO came; 32 of Bengal's 49 closest seats are places people leave to find work. Tribal and homeless people often have few documents at all. Maharashtra's chief electoral officer asked the EC to create a special category of documents for exactly these groups.

    Part No. 7 · 2026

    A new question on the form for new voters

    For anyone left off, the way back in is a form. And in 2026, that form changed. Form 6 is the form you fill in to get on the voter roll. It is used by 18-year-olds voting for the first time. It is also used by people who were removed and want to get back on. What goes in Form 6 is set by law, in the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960. Only the central government can change those Rules. But in early July 2026, the online version of Form 6, on the EC's ECINet website, got an extra section. There was no public announcement, and the paper form stayed the same.

    Below is a simple version of the form. The red box is the new section. Click one of its three options to see what it asks you to do.

    Think about who fills in this form. In a state where the SIR is over, "the last SIR roll" means the new roll. A first-time voter is not on it. So they must point to a parent or grandparent instead. But if that relative was among the crores removed (Parts 3 to 5), there is no link to show.

    There is a second problem, at the bottom of the form. Every Form 6 ends with a declaration that you sign. The EC's own guidelines warn about it. A false statement in it is a crime under Section 31 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950. The punishment can be up to one year in prison, a fine, or both. The new section was not marked as compulsory. But the online form would not submit until you filled it in.

    Put those together and some applicants are asked to sign things they cannot honestly sign. Here are two examples. The people are made up, but the form rules are as reported.

    The Commission says Form 6 itself was never changed. It says the new section is a separate, temporary step that applies only during an SIR. It also says the Supreme Court approved the SIR's document rules on 27 May 2026.

    Two of the Commission's own members disagreed, in writing, months before the public knew. Their objections became public only on 23 September. Within a week, the EC said the new section would apply only during an SIR. It removed the parent and grandparent questions from the online form in the 20 states and UTs where the SIR is over.

    Form 6 was only one of the things those two members objected to. That brings us back to where this story started: the Indian Express report.

    Part No. 8 · The breaking point, 23 September 2026

    Two against one

    To see why the Indian Express report of 23 September mattered so much, you first need to know how the Commission makes decisions. The Election Commission is not one person. It has three members: the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and two Election Commissioners. The law asks them to agree on decisions where possible. When they disagree, the majority decides. The Supreme Court has called the CEC "first among equals", not a boss. Click on each member to learn about them.

    The report said Sandhu and Joshi had formally objected, in writing, at least 14 times in 10 months. They objected to SIR decisions and orders that were issued without their knowledge or advice. On one day alone, they filed four objections. Remember, two members out of three are a majority of the Commission.

    The figure below shows the 14 objections as slips of paper. Under them is everything reports have said about individual objections: five with dates, and three without. You don't need to click anything. The count starts in November 2025, but none of the objections described so far is from before April 2026.

    The Form 6 fight made the headlines. But a second issue may matter more in the long run: who controls the voter database. Remember the deletion attempt in Aland, from Part 2. Who can remove a name from the roll is not a technical detail.

    Remember Part 1. By law, the ERO of each constituency decides whether to add or remove a name. In January 2026, the Commission moved all voter-roll work onto a new national software system called ECINet. According to the two commissioners' notes, this software let the Commission's IT department in Delhi control who could do what. EROs lost full access, even the power to fix wrong entries, and some names were added or removed centrally, from Delhi.

    Officials in the states reported the same problem:

    • Goa. One ERO sent eight emails in one week, complaining that he could not get access. Nothing changed.
    • West Bengal. Notices were sent out from Delhi in local officers' names, without the officers knowing.
    • Maharashtra. The state's chief electoral officer, S. Chockalingam, asked the Commission to give four decisions back to EROs. ECINet had taken them away. They were: which "discrepancy" cases need a hearing, which documents to accept, which mismatches EROs can fix themselves, and how checks are done. He also said spoken instructions from the EC were not enough. He wanted them in writing.

    The Commission says ECINet is safe, and that it does not put power in Delhi's hands. It has set up a committee to check that the software follows the law. A senior EC official leads the committee, and it includes an outside expert from an IIT or IIIT. The EC also says any new software process will go to this committee before it is approved.

    Part No. 9

    Can a Chief Election Commissioner be removed?

    Not easily, and that is on purpose. The Constitution protects the CEC in the same way it protects a Supreme Court judge. This is so that no government can sack a referee it does not like. The other two commissioners can be removed only if the CEC recommends it. Since a 2023 law, the CEC is chosen by a panel of three: the Prime Minister, a Union minister, and the Leader of the Opposition. The law left the Chief Justice of India off this panel. Gyanesh Kumar was the first CEC chosen under this law, in February 2025.

    The same law also gave commissioners a legal shield. The first draft of the law did not have it. Under Section 16, courts cannot hear civil or criminal cases against the CEC or election commissioners for anything done as part of their official duty. This protection continues even after they leave office. It also covers things done while "purporting to act" in that duty, which means while claiming to do the job. Critics say this makes the shield very wide. It does not cover crimes that have nothing to do with the job. It also does not directly mention arrest or police investigation. But it is the main legal barrier to opposition demands to arrest or prosecute Kumar over the voter rolls. This section was added about four months after the bill was first brought to Parliament in August 2023. An NGO called Lok Prahari has challenged it in the Supreme Court. In January 2026, the court asked the government and the EC to respond. But it did not put the section on hold.

    The opposition has already tried once to remove him. Here is how far it got.

    Since 24 September, there have been reports that opposition parties are preparing a new notice. This time they will use the commissioners' objections. They argue that if two of the three members objected in writing, the CEC went beyond his powers. But the new notice must still get past step 2. Resigning is the only faster way out, and that is what the protesters are demanding.

    "This is not an issue of ruling versus Opposition."Manoj Jha, RJD MP, announcing the Jantar Mantar protest

    BJP spokesperson Sambit Patra called the campaign a smear by parties that keep losing elections. The Commission says everything it did was legal and was approved by the Supreme Court.

    The next few weeks will test both sides. Here are the dates to watch.

    Few people say that India's voter rolls, last rebuilt over twenty years ago, did not need cleaning. The fight is about how it was done. Who has to prove that a voter belongs? What counts as a "discrepancy"? Can a form change without the law changing? And who controls the button that deletes a name? Today, two of the three people in charge of these questions have said, in writing, that they were not consulted.
